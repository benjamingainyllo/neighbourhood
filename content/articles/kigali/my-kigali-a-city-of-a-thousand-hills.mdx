When I was a child, my mother used to say you could tell a Kigali person by their calves. Everything here is up or down. Getting to school meant climbing; getting home meant a careful, knee-jarring descent. Nobody walks in a straight line in this city, and for a long time, neither did I.

I left at nineteen to study in Brussels and stayed for eleven years. When I came back, the city had rearranged itself around me: new roads, new towers, a convention centre lit up like a lantern at night. It took me a full year to stop feeling like a tourist in my own neighbourhood.

## The city in the morning

What brought me back to Kigali, in the end, was the mornings. The air is cool, the hills are soft with mist and the moto-taxis haven't yet started their buzzing. I walk from Kimihurura down to the valley and back up again, and by the time I reach the top I've usually forgotten whatever was worrying me.

## What visitors miss

People come for the gorillas and leave after a night in the city. I understand why, but they miss so much. The Saturday tailors of Nyamirambo. The coffee co-ops whose beans you'll find in London roasteries. The tiny galleries in converted houses where young artists show work about memory, rebuilding and joy.

<PullQuote cite="Aline Uwase">
  Kigali is not a city you see. It's a city you slowly begin to notice.
</PullQuote>

## How to spend a day like a local

Start with coffee and mandazi. Walk until lunch, eat brochettes and fried plantain at a roadside spot, then take a moto-taxi up to whichever hill has the best light that afternoon. In the evening, find a rooftop and watch the lights come on, one hill at a time.
